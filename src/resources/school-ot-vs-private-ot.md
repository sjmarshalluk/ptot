School-based occupational therapy and private occupational therapy sound like the same service delivered in two places. They aren't. They're built around different questions, and understanding that difference explains a lot of the confusion families run into.

## The one-sentence version

**School OT asks: is this getting in the way of the child accessing their education?**

**Private OT asks: is this getting in the way of the child's life?**

Everything else follows from that.

## What school-based OT covers

School OT is a related service under special education law. It exists to help a child access and make progress in their educational program. That's a real and valuable thing — but it's a bounded one.

In practice it means school OT tends to focus on:

- Handwriting, keyboarding, and the physical mechanics of schoolwork
- Managing classroom materials, desk set-up, and seating
- Sensory strategies that let a child stay regulated enough to learn
- Navigating the school building, the cafeteria, and playground equipment
- Self-care skills that come up during the school day

It's usually delivered in or around the classroom, often in a group, and often for short sessions. The therapist may be covering several schools.

## What it doesn't cover — by design, not by neglect

Because the legal test is educational access, school OT generally cannot address things that sit outside the school day, even when those are the things making family life hardest.

That includes mealtimes and feeding at home. Bedtime and sleep routines. Getting dressed and out the door in the morning. Bathing and hair washing. Sibling relationships and family play. Community outings. Regulation in the evening, after a day of holding it together.

This is the pattern I hear most often: a child who is coping at school — sometimes coping impressively — and coming home with nothing left. Teachers report no concerns. The family is exhausted. School OT is doing exactly what it's meant to do, and the hardest parts of the day are untouched by it.

## The eligibility gap

There's a second thing worth knowing. A child has to *qualify* for school-based OT, and the criteria are about educational impact rather than about difficulty.

So a child can be:

- Doing fine academically but struggling badly with regulation at home
- Below the district's threshold while still having real difficulties
- On a waiting list for evaluation for months
- Attending a school without an OT on staff

None of those mean the child wouldn't benefit from occupational therapy. They mean the child didn't meet a specific legal test.

## Why families end up doing both

The two fit together well when they're coordinated:

- **School OT** handles the classroom — handwriting, seating, the sensory demands of the building
- **Private OT** handles home and community — mealtimes, routines, regulation, play, family life

Private OT also isn't capped by educational relevance, doesn't require eligibility, and can begin without a wait. In my own practice, sessions happen [in the home](/in-home-ot/) — in person, or remotely by video — which is exactly where the school-day gap tends to show up.

## Making them work together

If your child has school OT and you're adding private OT, a few things help:

**Tell both.** Neither will mind, and both will do better work knowing about the other.

**Sign a release.** With your permission, the two therapists can talk directly. Ten minutes of that is worth a lot of guessing.

**Divide the ground deliberately.** Duplicating goals wastes your money. Agree who's holding what.

**Bring the school's evaluation.** If your child has been assessed by the district, that's useful information — including the parts that led to a finding of ineligibility.

I'm glad to coordinate with school teams, contribute to IEP discussions, or simply compare notes with a school-based therapist. Working alongside what's already in place is normal, not a complication.
